Starting IFS on Your Own

A short guide to the best starting materials, a first practice, and worksheets to use as you go.

Internal Family Systems (IFS) is a therapeutic model for exploring your inner experience. This brief is for people who want to explore it on their own.

The idea in one minute

IFS offers a way to understand a familiar experience: you are not one voice in your head. Part of you wants to relax, another part is anxious about work, another is harshly critical. IFS calls these parts, and treats them as normal, not as a disorder.

Within this model, underneath the parts is what IFS calls your Self: the calm, curious, steady you that can listen to all of them. The goal is not to get rid of parts. It is to let Self lead, so the parts can stop working so hard.

Managers
Parts that run daily life and try to prevent pain: the planner, the perfectionist, the inner critic.
Firefighters
Parts that react when pain breaks through, often with something that numbs or distracts: overeating, scrolling, drinking, rage.
Exiles
Young, hurt parts carrying fear or shame, usually pushed out of sight by the other two.
Blending
When a part’s feelings take over and feel like you. “I’m furious” instead of “a part of me is furious.”
Unblending
Creating a little room so you can notice a part and have access to your own perspective too. It does not mean suppressing or pushing the part away. This is the core skill, and most of what you will practice.

Definitions follow the IFS Institute’s model outline and glossary.

How to pace it

Start with everyday material: a recurring irritation, a procrastination habit, an inner critic, a stress response. The model itself favors going slowly and letting parts set the pace.

What shows up in a session is an impression from a part, not necessarily a record of what happened. Images and stories are worth listening to, but don’t treat them as proof of a memory.

A simple starting path

  1. Read the one-minute introduction above or watch the five-minute introduction by Richard Schwartz.
  2. Open the starter workbook below. Begin with the Parts Inventory and Parts Map.
  3. Use the workbook’s Unblending Checklist to get to know one protective part. The short practice below follows the same general progression.
  4. Record what you notice in a Profile Card, then use the Self Energy and 8 Cs check-in to reflect over time.

You do not need to buy a book before starting with these materials. Parts may show up as thoughts, feelings, urges, sensations, or images; distinct voices or characters are not required.

Your first practice

Choose something small, like the part of you that procrastinates or the one that criticizes your work. Skip your most painful memory. Start with a few minutes somewhere quiet, with a notebook. This is an introductory practice for getting to know a protective part, rather than a complete account of IFS therapy.

  1. Settle and pick one part.Take a few slow breaths. Bring to mind the situation where this part shows up.
  2. Notice where it lives.Where do you feel it: a tight chest, a knot in the stomach, a voice, an image? Any of these counts.
  3. Make a little space.Say to yourself, “I notice a part of me that…” Ask it to step back slightly so you can talk. This is unblending.
  4. Check how you feel toward it.If you feel curious or at least open, continue. If you feel annoyed, afraid, or want it gone, IFS invites you to explore whether another part is reacting. Acknowledge that reaction and gently ask whether it is willing to make room. You can stay with its concerns or finish here; there is no need to repeat the steps until you get a particular result.
  5. Ask and listen.What does this part want you to know? What is it trying to do for you? What would it be afraid of if it stopped? Don’t argue with the answers. Write down whatever comes.
  6. Thank it and close.Appreciate what it does for you, tell it you’ll check in again, and take a few breaths before returning to your day. Jot a note on what you learned.

If it isn’t working

  • A part does not want to step back. Respect that. You can ask what concerns it about making space, stay with simply noticing, or close the exercise. Agreement is not a test of success.
  • Nothing comes. That’s common. Try writing instead of picturing, or notice the body sensation only. Don’t force it.
  • Another part keeps interrupting. Pause and address the interrupter first. It usually has a worry about the process.
  • You want the part to change. That wanting can be explored as a part. IFS asks you to understand a part before asking anything of it.
  • It’s going too fast. Ask the part to signal when it’s too much, and respect that signal. Slowing down is part of the method.

What the evidence says

IFS has promising early results but a smaller research base than long-established therapies. The IFS Institute’s own research page describes the evidence as emerging. Small trials show benefit for depression and PTSD, and several studies show increased self-compassion. Reductions in depression are the only condition that has shown significant improvement across multiple pilot trials. A first randomized trial of an IFS-based PTSD group program was published in 2026 (Joss et al.). The Institute also notes that the risk of harm has not been systematically studied.

For a view from outside the Institute, see the 2025 scoping review by Buys. Most of this research tests IFS delivered by trained clinicians, not self-guided practice.
